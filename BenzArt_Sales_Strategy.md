## 0. A note on method

The source sales skill is built for B2B outbound prospecting — BANT/MEDDIC scoring, buying committees, cold email sequences. BenzArt is not an outbound business. It is a **made-to-order commission studio selling a single high-consideration purchase to inbound visitors**, most of whom arrive from organic search, Pinterest, or Instagram.

So the transferable parts are used and the rest is discarded:

| Skill component | Applied? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ICP definition | Yes, adapted | Firmographic → household/project profile |
| Negative ICP | Yes, in full | The highest-leverage section for this business |
| Buyer personas | Yes | Drives all site copy |
| Objection handling (FFF/ABC) | Yes | Becomes FAQ + inquiry-page copy |
| Lead scoring rubric | Yes, simplified | For triaging inbound enquiries |
| Cold outreach sequences | No | No outbound motion exists |
| Buying committee mapping | Partially | Only for the trade/contract segment |

**Every number in this document is a stated assumption, not a measured fact.** BenzArt has no analytics history, no CRM, and no closed-won data yet. These are calibration starting points to be replaced with real figures after the first ~20 enquiries. They are marked ⚠️ where they matter most.

## 1. What is actually being sold

Not a table. Three things, in this order:

1. **Certainty** — that spending €4,000–€15,000 on an object you have never seen, from a workshop 2,000km away, will end well.
2. **Participation** — you chose the slab, the colour, the dimensions. It is yours in a way a showroom purchase is not.
3. **The object** — the table itself.

Almost every lost sale is a failure of (1). The competitor is not another maker; it is **the visitor deciding to buy something safe from a showroom instead**. Strategy follows from that.

### Positioning statement

> For people furnishing a room they care about, BenzArt is the Marrakech atelier that makes one table at a time — where you choose the actual slab before anything is cut, and the price is fixed before work begins.

## 2. Ideal Customer Profile

### 2.1 Primary segment — the private commission

| Criteria | Ideal | Why it matters | Red flag if missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Budget | €4,000–€15,000 ⚠️ | Below €3,000 the slab economics don't work | "What's your cheapest table?" |
| Trigger | Renovation, move, extension in last 6 months | Commissioning needs an occasion | No stated occasion → browsing |
| Room | Known dimensions, room already exists | Constrains the slab search immediately | "Not sure what size yet" |
| Timeline | 3–9 months out | 8–12 week build + freight | "Need it in 3 weeks" |
| Geography | France, UK, Benelux, Gulf, Morocco, US East | Freight cost and customs sanity | Landlocked remote destinations |
| Decision | 1–2 people, both reachable | Third-party approval kills momentum | "I'll ask my wife" with no intro |
| Prior behaviour | Has bought bespoke before (kitchen, joinery, art) | Understands lead times and deposits | Expects Amazon delivery |

**Highest-value signal:** the visitor sends **room dimensions unprompted**. In a made-to-order business this is the single strongest intent marker — it costs the visitor effort and only serious buyers spend it.

### 2.2 Secondary segment — trade and contract

Interior designers, architects, restaurant/hotel groups, corporate fit-outs.

Different economics: lower emotion, higher volume, longer cycle, and a real buying committee (specifier → project manager → finance). Repeat business is the prize — one designer can be worth ten private clients.

Key differences in handling:
- They need **CAD dimensions, lead-time guarantees, and payment terms in writing** — not storytelling
- They ask about **replaceability**, which we cannot offer honestly (see §5, Objection 7)
- Spec changes to film finish and conservative dimensions (already documented in the blog cluster)

### 2.3 Negative ICP — who to decline

The most valuable section here. Declining early protects margin and reputation.

1. **Budget under €3,000** — the slab alone consumes it. Say so immediately; don't quote.
2. **Deadline under 8 weeks** — resin cure time is chemistry, not scheduling. Never promise it.
3. **Outdoor or conservatory placement** — UV ambers resin, humidity stresses the interface. Decline, explain, offer an indoor alternative.
4. **"Can you copy this exact photo?"** — every slab is unique. If they want an identical replica, they want a factory.
5. **High-volume casual dining** — plates straight onto tables, no service standards. The material will disappoint them.
6. **Needs identical replacements on demand** — fundamentally incompatible with one-of-a-kind. Be blunt.
7. **Won't share destination country** — freight and duty are 10–25% of landed cost. No destination, no quote.
8. **Pushing for under-declared customs value** — illegal, voids insurance, penalties land on them as importer. Decline the whole job.
9. **Wants the price before the brief** — signals price-shopping, not commissioning.
10. **Hostile to the deposit structure** — 50% secures a specific slab that is then unsellable to anyone else. Non-negotiable.

Publishing several of these openly (in the FAQ) is itself a qualifying mechanism: it repels the wrong enquiries before they cost anyone time.

## 3. Buyer personas

### Persona A — "The Renovator" (primary, ~60% of private enquiries ⚠️)

Mid-40s, finishing an 18-month renovation. The dining room is the last unfinished space and they refuse to fill it with something ordinary. Has spent months on Pinterest. Financially comfortable but not careless — this is a considered purchase, not an impulse.

**Their words:** *"I've been looking for months and everything is either mass-produced or completely out of budget."*

**Goal:** one table that makes the room finished.

**Fears, in their order:**
1. It arrives and the colour is wrong
2. It arrives damaged and cannot be replaced
3. It doesn't fit through the door
4. They get quoted one price and charged another

**What wins them:** photographs of the actual slab before cutting; a fixed quote; the crating explanation; an honest access-route conversation.

**What loses them:** vague pricing, stock photography, unanswered emails, any hint of a moving price.

### Persona B — "The Designer" (trade, ~25% ⚠️)

Specifies on behalf of clients. Their professional reputation rides on the piece arriving correctly and on time. Emotionally detached from the object, intensely attached to the schedule.

**Their words:** *"I need to know it lands the week of the install, and I need that in writing."*

**Goal:** a specification-grade supplier they can reuse.

**Fears:** slipping the install date; being blamed for a customs delay; a supplier who won't answer the phone mid-project.

**What wins them:** CAD-ready dimensions, written lead times, named contact, trade terms, honest "no" when a date isn't achievable.

**What loses them:** artistic vagueness, over-promising on dates, no documentation.

### Persona C — "The Collector" (~15% ⚠️)

Buys objects because they are singular. Least price-sensitive, most taste-sensitive. Wants provenance and the story of the tree.

**Their words:** *"Where did the slab come from? Is this the only one?"*

**What wins them:** the wood's origin, the maker's name, the irreproducibility, sculpted/textured work.

**What loses them:** anything that smells like a product line, tiered "collections", or discounting.

## 4. Inbound lead scoring

Not the 100-point B2B rubric — a 60-second triage for enquiries arriving through the form.

| Signal | Points |
|---|---|
| Gave room dimensions | 25 |
| Gave a budget range | 20 |
| Gave a destination country | 15 |
| Stated an occasion/trigger (move, reno) | 15 |
| Timeline ≥ 3 months | 10 |
| Arrived via a table detail page (`?ref=`) | 10 |
| Trade/repeat signal | 5 |

**A (75+)** — respond within 24h with slab options.
**B (50–74)** — respond within 48h with one clarifying question first.
**C (25–49)** — send the commissioning guide, ask for dimensions.
**D (<25)** — polite reply, link to FAQ, no manual effort.

The inquiry form already captures every one of these fields, so scoring is mechanical.

**60-second check:** Do they have a room? A budget? A date? A country? A reason? Five yes = A.

## 5. Objection handling

These map directly onto site copy. Each is written for the visitor's actual words.

### Objection 1 — "It's expensive"

*Real meaning:* they cannot see what the money buys.

**FFF:** "I understand — it's a significant amount for a table. Most people who commission with us felt the same at first; they'd seen similar-looking pieces for a third of the price. What they found was that the slab alone was most of the difference — a board wide enough for a 110cm top comes from a rarer tree than one giving 85cm, and that's the number that moves. Would it help if I showed you exactly where the money goes?"

**ABC:** "That's fair, and I'd rather talk about it directly than dance around it. *(A)* The price is mostly the slab and the hand-finishing — the two things that also make it last thirty years instead of five. *(B)* Our pricing breakdown is published; if the maths doesn't justify it for your room, I'll tell you so. *(C)*"

**Follow-up:** "Is it higher than you budgeted, or higher than you expected? Those are different problems."

**Walk away when:** the budget is genuinely under €3,000. Say so kindly and immediately.

### Objection 2 — "How do I know the colour will be right?"

*Real meaning:* fear of an irreversible mistake.

**ABC:** "That's the right thing to worry about — screens lie. *(A)* Which is why we don't ask you to approve a colour on a screen: we mix samples and photograph them against your actual slab, in daylight. *(B)* Nothing is poured until you've seen that. *(C)*"

**Follow-up:** "Do you have a fabric or paint reference you're matching to?"

### Objection 3 — "What if it arrives damaged?"

**FFF:** "It's the thing we worry about most too, because your table cannot be remade — the slab no longer exists. Clients arriving from overseas felt the same. What they found reassuring was the crating: it's built around the specific piece from ISPM 15 certified timber, braced so nothing shifts, since movement rather than impact causes most transit damage. We also send an inspection checklist for the moment it arrives."

**Follow-up:** "What's the narrowest point on the route into the room? Doorways defeat more deliveries than freight does."

### Objection 4 — "8 to 12 weeks is a long time"

**ABC:** "It is, and I won't pretend otherwise. *(A)* Most of it is cure time between resin layers — that's chemistry and can't be compressed by working longer hours. Rushing it is exactly how tables crack in year two. *(B)* If you tell me your date now, I'll tell you honestly whether it's achievable before you commit. *(C)*"

**Walk away when:** the deadline is under 8 weeks and immovable. Decline. A missed date costs more than the sale.

### Objection 5 — "I've never bought from Morocco"

*Real meaning:* distance risk — recourse if it goes wrong.

**ABC:** "Completely reasonable — you're buying from a workshop you can't walk into. *(A)* So we make the process visible: photographs at slab selection, during the pour, and before crating; a fixed quote; the balance not due until it ships. *(B)* You're never asked to pay for something you haven't seen progress on. *(C)*"

**Follow-up:** "Would progress photos at each stage make this easier?"

### Objection 6 — "Can I see one in person?"

Honest answer: only in Marrakech, by appointment. Do not manufacture alternatives. Offer: detailed photography, video of the actual slab, and a sample offcut of the wood-and-resin finish posted to them. **That last one is worth more than any amount of copy** — it converts the abstract into something they hold.

### Objection 7 — "What if it gets damaged in five years?" *(trade especially)*

Do not oversell. "Resin surfaces can be refinished and hard-wax oil can be spot-repaired. But an identical replacement isn't possible — the slab is gone. If your project needs guaranteed identical replacements, commission the spare now, or this isn't the right material for you." Losing this deal honestly is better than winning it and being blamed later.

### Objection 8 — "Let me think about it"

**ABC:** "Of course. *(A)* In my experience that usually comes down to one or two specific unknowns rather than general hesitation. *(B)* What's the one thing that, if you knew it for certain, would make this straightforward? *(C)*"

Never chase more than twice. This is a considered purchase; pressure reads as desperation and destroys the premium positioning.

## 6. Conversion path

The site's job, stage by stage:

| Stage | Visitor question | Page | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discover | "Does this exist?" | Blog, Pinterest, Instagram | Prove the craft is real |
| Evaluate | "Are these people any good?" | `/tables`, `/craftsmanship` | Show slabs and process |
| Justify | "What does it cost, and why?" | Pricing article, `/faq` | Remove price anxiety |
| De-risk | "What if it goes wrong?" | Shipping + ISPM 15 articles | Answer fears before asked |
| Commit | "How do I start?" | `/inquiry` | Make step one feel small |

**The critical transition is Justify → De-risk.** Most drop-off happens where price is understood but risk is not. The published pricing breakdown, the crating detail, and the fixed-quote promise all exist to carry visitors across that gap.

### Structural recommendations

1. **Make the first step smaller.** "Submit Inquiry" asks for commitment. "See which slabs suit your room" asks for curiosity. Same form, lower barrier.
2. **Sample offcut offer** — the single highest-leverage addition available. Cheap, tangible, and it kills the "never bought from Morocco" objection outright.
3. **Progress photography as a stated deliverable**, not a favour. It is the entire answer to distance risk.
4. **Publish the negative ICP** in the FAQ. Repelling wrong-fit enquiries is a feature.
5. **Trade page** — the secondary segment has different needs and currently has nowhere to land.
6. **Never discount.** For Persona C it actively destroys value. If budget is short, reduce scope (narrower top, simpler base, single-pigment pour) — never the price of the same object.

## 7. What to measure

Since nothing is measured today, start here:

- Enquiry rate by landing page
- % of enquiries with dimensions supplied (proxy for lead quality)
- Enquiry → quote → deposit conversion at each step
- Time-to-first-response (the strongest controllable variable in high-consideration sales)
- Loss reasons, recorded verbatim — after 20, this document gets rewritten from evidence

**Review this document once 20 real enquiries exist.** Everything marked ⚠️ is a guess until then.

## 8. Copy implications

Voice, derived from the above and applied across `/en`, `/fr`, `/ar`:

- **Second person, always.** "Your room", "your slab", not "clients receive".
- **Name the fear before they do.** Pre-empting an objection converts better than answering it.
- **Specific over grand.** "240 × 100cm, eight seats, twelve weeks" beats "timeless elegance".
- **No superlatives, no exclamation marks.** Premium is conveyed by precision, not volume.
- **Honest limits stated plainly.** "We can't do outdoor" builds more trust than any claim.
- **Every page ends with one clear, low-commitment next step.**
