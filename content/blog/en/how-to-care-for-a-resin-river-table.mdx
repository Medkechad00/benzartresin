Resin tables are not fragile, but they are specific. Almost everything that goes wrong with them comes from three causes: heat, UV, and the wrong cleaning product.

Get those right and maintenance is genuinely minimal.

## Daily cleaning

A soft, damp cloth. That is the whole routine.

For anything greasier, a drop of mild pH-neutral soap in warm water, then wipe dry. Do not leave the surface wet — standing liquid is the main enemy of the wood-resin interface, which is the one place where the two materials meet and can be worked apart over time.

**Avoid entirely:**

- Abrasive cloths, scouring pads, and cream cleansers — resin scratches more easily than glass
- Anything containing ammonia, bleach, or solvent (most glass cleaners and many all-purpose sprays)
- Silicone-based furniture polish, which builds a haze that is unpleasant to remove and interferes with any future refinishing
- Alcohol-based wipes and hand sanitiser, which can dull the finish

## Heat

This is the one that ruins tables.

Epoxy is a thermoset plastic. It softens at temperatures well below the bottom of a pan that has just come off a hob. Because the resin is the structural river rather than a coating, heat damage is not a mark on the surface — it is a permanent depression in the material.

There is no repair for this short of re-machining the top.

Use trivets, always. Also keep the table away from radiators and off underfloor heating, where sustained moderate heat causes the wood and resin to move against each other.

## Sunlight

All epoxy ambers under UV. Deep-pour formulations include inhibitors and quality resin resists it well, but nothing is permanently UV-stable.

The mitigation is placement, not product. A table that spends every afternoon in direct sun will change noticeably faster than one in indirect light.

If the position is unavoidable, sheer window film or blinds during peak hours do more than anything you could apply to the surface. Note also that partial coverage — a runner, a bowl left in the same place for years — can produce uneven ageing, which is more visible than uniform ambering.

## Spills

Wipe promptly. Water, wine, coffee, and citrus are all fine if dealt with reasonably quickly.

Standing liquid is the problem, particularly at the wood-resin boundary. Prolonged moisture there can work the interface apart over years.

Watch for oils. Cooking oil and some cosmetics can darken raw or oil-finished wood if left. Wipe and dry.

## Humidity and movement

Wood responds to humidity; resin does not. Large seasonal swings put stress on the joint between them.

Aim for reasonably stable indoor humidity. In very dry heated winters, general room humidification helps the table as much as it helps you. Extreme dryness is what opens hairline separations at the interface.

## Long-term maintenance

**Hard-wax oil finishes** need periodic reapplication — typically every one to two years depending on use. This is straightforward, and the significant advantage is that scratches and water rings can be spot-repaired without refinishing the whole table.

**Polyurethane finishes** need nothing until they fail, and then need full refinishing. They resist more, but they cannot be repaired locally.

Which yours has depends on what was specified — [epoxy versus polyurethane](/blog/epoxy-vs-polyurethane-table-finish) explains the trade-off.

## Fixing minor damage

**Light scratches in an oil finish:** clean, sand the local area very lightly with fine abrasive, re-oil, blend outward. This works well.

**Scratches in resin:** progressive polishing compounds can improve surface scratches. Anything deep should be left to someone who has done it before — resin polishes unevenly and it is easy to create a visible dished patch.

**Scratches in polyurethane:** these generally cannot be spot-repaired invisibly. Live with them, or refinish.

**Water rings:** usually a finish problem rather than a wood problem, and usually recoverable in an oil finish.

**Heat damage:** not repairable. Prevention only.

## After delivery

Let the piece acclimatise for a few days before heavy use, especially after a long international shipment. Wood that has crossed climates needs to settle. [Shipping and arrival](/blog/international-shipping-and-handling) covers what to check on delivery.

## The short version

Damp cloth, trivets, out of direct sun, wipe spills, don't use glass cleaner. That is essentially all of it.
