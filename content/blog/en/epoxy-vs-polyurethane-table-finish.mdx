This question comes up constantly, and it is usually framed as a choice. It mostly is not one.

**Epoxy is a casting resin.** It fills the void, forms the river, and becomes part of the table's structure. It is poured in depth.

**Polyurethane is a coating.** It goes on in thin films to protect the surface. It cannot fill a void.

On a river table the epoxy is already there by definition. The real question is what goes over the top.

## The three surface options

### Hard-wax oil

Penetrates the wood rather than forming a film. Cures within the timber, leaving a low-sheen, natural surface that feels like wood rather than plastic.

**Strengths:** repairable in place. A scratch or a water ring can be spot-sanded and re-oiled without refinishing the whole table — for a one-of-a-kind piece that is a significant advantage. It also ages gracefully rather than failing suddenly.

**Weaknesses:** less resistant to standing liquid and needs periodic reapplication, typically every year or two depending on use.

### Polyurethane

Forms a protective film on the surface. Available in oil-based and water-based formulations, in a range of sheens.

**Strengths:** genuinely tough. Better resistance to standing water, alcohol, and abrasion than an oil. Water-based versions stay clear rather than ambering.

**Weaknesses:** when it fails, it fails visibly — scratches show as white lines in the film and cannot be spot-repaired invisibly. Repair means sanding back and recoating the whole surface. Oil-based versions yellow over time, which is unwelcome over pale timber like maple.

### Epoxy flood coat

A thin layer of epoxy poured across the entire top as a finish.

**Strengths:** very high build, glassy, and highly water-resistant.

**Weaknesses:** the least UV-stable of the three, the most prone to visible scratching, essentially impossible to repair locally, and it gives the surface a thick plastic look that many buyers dislike on a piece whose appeal is natural material. We rarely recommend it.

## How they compare

| | Hard-wax oil | Polyurethane | Epoxy flood |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feel | Natural, tactile | Slight film | Thick, glassy |
| Water resistance | Good | Very good | Excellent |
| Scratch visibility | Low | High | High |
| Spot repairable | Yes | No | No |
| UV stability | Good | Good (water-based) | Poor |
| Heat tolerance | Poor | Poor | Poor |
| Maintenance | Periodic re-oil | None until refinish | None until refinish |

## Heat: the honest answer

None of these survive a hot pan. Epoxy in particular softens at temperatures well below what comes out of an oven, and because it is the structural river material, heat damage there is not a surface problem — it is permanent deformation.

Use trivets. This is not finish-specific advice; it applies to every resin table ever made.

## UV: the other honest answer

All epoxy ambers with UV exposure. Quality deep-pour formulations include UV inhibitors that slow it considerably, but nothing stops it entirely.

The most effective mitigation is placement. A table in sustained direct sunlight will change faster than one that is not, regardless of which topcoat you choose. [Care and placement](/blog/how-to-care-for-a-resin-river-table) covers this.

## What we recommend

**Private dining rooms:** hard-wax oil, in most cases. The repairability matters more over decades than the marginal extra resistance of a film finish, and the surface feels better.

**Households with young children, or heavy daily use:** water-based polyurethane. The added resistance to spills and abrasion is worth losing spot repair.

**Restaurants and hospitality:** polyurethane, without much hesitation. Commercial use is a different problem entirely — see [whether resin tables can handle restaurant service](/blog/can-resin-tables-handle-hospitality-restaurant-use).

**Reception desks and corporate installations:** polyurethane, for the same reasons.

## The thing that matters more than the finish

Preparation. A well-prepared surface under an average finish outperforms a poorly-prepared one under an excellent finish, every time.
