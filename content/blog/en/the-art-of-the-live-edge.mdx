A live edge is the outer profile of the tree, retained rather than sawn straight. It is the single most recognisable feature of this kind of furniture and the most commonly misunderstood.

## What it actually is

When a log is milled into boards, the outermost cut retains the tree's natural outline — the undulation of the trunk, the swell around a branch, the taper toward the crown.

That contour is a record of how the tree grew. A bulge marks a limb; a compression is where the tree responded to load or to a neighbour; a scar is an old wound the tree grew around. None of it is decorative in origin, which is precisely why it reads as authentic in a way that a machined profile cannot.

## The bark question

Most people picture a live edge with bark still attached. In practice, bark almost always comes off, and it should.

Three reasons:

**It separates.** Bark and the wood beneath dry and move at different rates. The bond between them fails — not immediately, but reliably. Bark retained on a finished table tends to lift and flake within a few years.

**It harbours insects.** The layer between bark and wood is exactly where wood-boring insects live. Retaining it means retaining the risk.

**What is underneath is better.** Removing bark exposes the cambium layer — the growth boundary — which is smooth, tight, and often a different colour from both the bark and the heartwood. Finished properly it is far more beautiful than bark ever was.

There are exceptions. Some species hold bark tenaciously, and a piece intended as sculpture rather than furniture may justify the risk. For a dining table that must survive decades, we remove it.

## Cleaning back

Bark removal is not the end of the preparation. The edge is worked back to sound material — soft spots, punky sections, and insect damage are cut away rather than filled and hoped over.

This changes the profile. An edge cleaned back properly is slightly different from the raw slab, and that is the correct outcome. Preserving a rotten contour for authenticity's sake produces a table with a rotten edge.

Where voids remain by design, they are consolidated and often filled with resin — which is where the live edge and the river meet as a single idea rather than two features. [The full craftsmanship process](/blog/epoxy-resin-live-edge-wood-craftsmanship-guide) covers stabilisation in detail.

## The edge as a design decision

Live edges vary enormously, and the character of a slab's edge should drive the design.

**Gentle undulation** suits long dining tables. It reads as calm and does not fight the room.

**Dramatic irregularity** — deep bays, pronounced swells — makes a shorter piece far more interesting. It is often better on a coffee table or console where the whole outline is visible at once than on a dining table where half of it is hidden by chairs.

**Matched pairs** are the classic river configuration: two boards from the same log, opened like a book, with the resin running between the two facing live edges. When the halves genuinely match, the effect is that the river was cut *through* a single piece of wood. This requires both halves to have been kept together from milling, which is why sourcing matters.

**Single-edge** designs keep the live edge on one long side only and take the other side straight — practical against a wall, and considerably easier to source since it does not require a matched pair.

## Under clear resin

A live edge encased in transparent resin is visible from above, from the side, and refracted through the pour. Every surface is on display.

This raises the standard of preparation substantially. Any tooling mark, any missed soft spot, any unsealed section that out-gassed during the pour will be permanently visible and magnified. It is the most demanding way to present an edge and the most rewarding.

Under an opaque pour, none of it shows. That is a legitimate design choice, but it does mean the feature you are paying for has been buried — worth thinking about before choosing [an opaque resin](/blog/resin-customization-colors-and-finishes).

## Species and edge character

[Walnut, olive, and maple](/blog/choosing-your-wood-walnut-vs-olive-vs-maple) produce quite different edges. Olive is wild and irregular. Walnut tends to gentler contours with striking sapwood at the boundary. Maple is fine-grained and shows the cambium line cleanly.
