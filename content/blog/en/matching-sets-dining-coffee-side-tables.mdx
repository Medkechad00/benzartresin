"Matching" means something specific in this work, and it is not what most people assume.

You cannot order a dining table now and a matching coffee table in two years. The material that would have matched no longer exists.

## Why matching is a sourcing problem

Timber colour, grain, and figure are properties of an individual tree — and of where in that tree a board was cut. Two walnut slabs from different trees can differ more than walnut differs from another species.

Genuine continuity therefore requires boards from the **same log**, ideally cut sequentially. Those boards share colour, density, grain direction, and figure, because they were adjacent in the same trunk.

This means the decision has to be made before any cutting begins. Once a log has been broken up and its boards sold, the set cannot be reassembled.

## What "matching" can actually mean

Four levels, in descending order of difficulty:

### 1. Sequential boards from one log

The highest standard. Boards cut in order from the same trunk, kept together. Grain runs continuously from one piece to the next, and figure that appears at the end of one board reappears at the start of the next.

This produces genuinely remarkable sets — the room reads as furnished from a single tree, because it is. It requires the whole log to be committed at the outset.

### 2. Same log, non-sequential

Boards from one trunk but not adjacent. Very close in colour and character, without continuous grain. This is the practical standard for most sets and is usually indistinguishable in a real room.

### 3. Matched species and treatment

Different trees, same species, same [resin treatment](/blog/resin-customization-colors-and-finishes) and finish. Pieces read as a family rather than a set. Perfectly good in most interiors, and much less constrained on sourcing and timing.

### 4. Deliberate contrast

Different species, unified by resin colour or by base design. Not matching at all — coordinating. Frequently the better answer, and we recommend it more often than clients expect.

## Why contrast often wins

Three pieces of identical dark walnut with identical black resin in one room can read as heavy and monotonous.

The same room with a walnut dining table, an [olive](/blog/choosing-your-wood-walnut-vs-olive-vs-maple) coffee table, and a maple console — all sharing a single resin tone and finish — has hierarchy. The dining table remains the anchor; the others support it.

Unify on **one** variable, and let the others vary. Resin colour and finish are the easiest to hold constant; species and scale are the most rewarding to vary.

## Practical guidance by piece

**Dining table:** the anchor. Specify it first and let everything else respond. [Sizing and proportion](/blog/designing-a-statement-dining-table) covers the constraints.

**Coffee table:** seen from above and from seated height, and the whole outline is visible. This is where a [dramatic live edge](/blog/the-art-of-the-live-edge) pays off — far more than on a dining table where chairs hide most of the perimeter.

**Console and side tables:** narrow, often against a wall, frequently a single live edge with the back taken straight. Efficient use of narrower boards, which makes them a good home for offcuts from a larger commission.

**Benches:** if you are seating people on a bench at the dining table, matching genuinely matters here — it sits directly alongside the anchor piece.

## Commercial sets

For hospitality and corporate work the calculation changes.

Where a scheme requires many similar tables, plan for attrition. If one of twenty is damaged in year two, a replacement from a different log will be visibly different. Commission spares at the outset — it is cheaper than the alternative and the material will not be available later.

[Hospitality specification](/blog/can-resin-tables-handle-hospitality-restaurant-use) covers the other commercial differences, and [reception and boardroom work](/blog/custom-epoxy-reception-desks-for-offices) raises the same sourcing question at larger scale.

## Cost and timing

Committing to a whole log means committing capital to material earlier. It can be more economical per piece — you are buying the trunk rather than selected boards — but it requires the full set to be decided up front.

Timing also compounds. Multiple pieces means multiple pours and multiple finishing cycles. A set does not take three times as long as one table, but it takes meaningfully longer than one. [the custom table timeline](/blog/from-consultation-to-shipping-timeline) explains the stages.

## What to do

If there is any chance you will want a second or third piece, say so at the first enquiry. We can reserve material against future pieces at the point of sourcing.

That conversation costs nothing now and is impossible later.
