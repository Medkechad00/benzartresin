The standard river table is a flat plane: two live edges, resin between, everything sanded level. It is the right answer most of the time.

But resin is a material, not just a filler, and it can be shaped.

## Why flat is the default

Flatness is functional. A dining surface needs to be level for plates and glasses, and it needs to be easy to wipe.

It is also efficient. A flat top is machined level after cure and sanded as a single plane. [The standard process](/blog/epoxy-resin-live-edge-wood-craftsmanship-guide) is built around that assumption.

Departing from it means hand work, and hand work on cured epoxy is slow.

## Contoured edges

The simplest departure, and the most broadly useful.

Instead of taking the outer edge square, the profile is shaped — a soft radius, a chamfer that follows the live edge, or a hand-worked contour that thins toward the perimeter. The top stays flat and functional; the perimeter gains a hand-made quality that a square edge does not have.

On a thick top this changes the piece considerably. A 6cm slab with a square edge reads as heavy; the same slab with a contoured underside reads as substantial but not blunt.

## Sculpted rivers

The resin channel itself is carved after cure, so the river has depth and topography rather than being a flat inlay.

Done well, this reinforces what the river is meant to evoke — water has a surface that is not perfectly flat, and a slightly dished or rippled channel catches light in a way a flat one cannot. Combined with a [tinted transparent pour](/blog/resin-customization-colors-and-finishes), the varying depth creates genuine colour gradation.

Done badly it is a puddle collector. The contour has to be shallow and continuous, and the polish has to be as good as the flat surface or the difference is glaring.

## Textured resin

Texture is introduced either during the pour — by manipulating the surface as it gels — or after cure by carving and abrading.

The important constraint is placement. Texture across the eating surface of a dining table is a mistake: it traps crumbs, it is unpleasant under a plate, and it is very hard to clean. Texture on a bar front, a reception desk fascia, a console face, or an [office reception installation](/blog/custom-epoxy-reception-desks-for-offices) is a different matter — those are vertical or non-working surfaces where tactility is an asset.

## Dimensional and layered pours

Multiple discrete pours at different depths and different pigment loadings, producing apparent strata within the resin.

This is one of the slowest things we do. Each layer must fully reach the right state before the next, and any error is entombed. But the result has genuine depth — the eye reads layers below the surface rather than a single coloured mass, and it changes with viewing angle.

## The Moroccan connection

There is a long tradition of surface work in Moroccan craft — carved cedar, incised plaster, tadelakt, marquetry. All of it is about what happens when a hand tool meets a surface repeatedly and deliberately.

Applying that sensibility to a synthetic material is not a gimmick. The techniques for reading a surface, working it evenly, and knowing when to stop transfer directly. [Moroccan craftsmanship and modern resin](/blog/moroccan-craftsmanship-meets-modern-resin) covers this in more depth.

## What it costs

Sculpted work happens after full cure, entirely by hand, and every contour then has to be sanded through the full grit progression individually. There is no machine pass that shortcuts it.

Expect sculpted or heavily textured work to add substantially to the finishing stage — often doubling it. [How pricing is calculated](/blog/how-our-pricing-is-calculated) sets out where bench hours land in a quote.

It also extends the schedule, since finishing is already the longest stage of [the custom table timeline](/blog/from-consultation-to-shipping-timeline).

## When it is worth it

**Yes:** pieces where the object is the point — consoles, coffee tables, entrance pieces, reception installations, anything seen from all sides and touched in passing.

**Probably not:** a working dining table for a family of five, where flatness and cleanability matter more every single day than topography does.

**Middle ground:** contoured edges on an otherwise flat top. This is the option we recommend most often, because it gives the piece a hand-made character without compromising the surface you actually use.
