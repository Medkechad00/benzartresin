The most common mistake in commissioning a dining table is choosing dimensions from an image rather than from the room.

A table that looks magnificent in a photograph of a large open space will make a normal dining room unusable. Here are the numbers that actually matter.

## Start with the room, not the table

Measure the room. Then subtract the circulation space, and what is left is your maximum table footprint.

**Allow 90–120cm from the table edge to any wall or fixed furniture.** This is not generous, it is functional: a person needs roughly 75cm to push a chair back and stand, and more than that to walk behind someone who is seated.

If your room is 400cm long, a 280cm table leaves 60cm at each end. That is too tight. A 240cm table leaves 80cm — workable but snug. A 220cm table is comfortable.

It is always better to have a slightly smaller table in a room that works than a magnificent one you have to edge around.

## Width

**90–110cm suits most dining.** This gives each place setting adequate depth and leaves a usable centre channel for serving dishes.

Below 85cm, opposite place settings begin to interfere and there is no centre space. Above 120cm the table becomes hard to reach across, and conversation with the person opposite starts to feel remote.

Width also drives cost sharply. A single board wide enough for a 110cm top comes from a substantially rarer tree than one yielding 90cm — [how pricing is calculated](/blog/how-our-pricing-is-calculated) explains why this is the biggest lever on the quote.

## Length and seating

Allow roughly 60cm of table edge per person.

- 180cm — 6 people
- 220cm — 8 people
- 260cm — 8 comfortably, 10 at a squeeze
- 300cm — 10 people

If you seat people at the ends, add two. Whether that works depends on the base — which is the next question.

## Height

**74–76cm** is standard and there is little reason to depart from it. Standard dining chairs are built for it.

The thing to watch on a resin table is **apron clearance**. These tops are often thick — 5–6cm is common — and if the base intrudes, knee room disappears. You want at least 60cm from floor to the underside of the top at every seating position.

Thick tops are part of the aesthetic, but they eat into the space where legs go. Check it.

## Base design

The base determines where people can actually sit, and it is routinely under-considered.

**Trestle or A-frame:** two supports set in from the ends. Very stable. But anyone seated at the end may find a leg in the way, so the inset distance matters.

**Pedestal:** central support, maximum freedom for seating anywhere around the perimeter. Requires careful engineering on a long table to resist tipping.

**Four-leg:** traditional and predictable. Corner seats are compromised.

**Cantilever or single-side:** dramatic, and an engineering exercise given the weight of a resin top. Possible, expensive.

For a table that will regularly seat people at the ends, a pedestal or a well-inset trestle is usually the right answer.

## Shape

**Rectangular** is the most efficient use of a room and the most straightforward to source, since it works with a single board or a matched pair.

**Organic** — where the live edges define the outline rather than being trimmed straight — is the most striking and the least predictable. It seats fewer people than its footprint suggests, because the outline narrows in places. It also demands a room with space around it, since the shape needs to be readable.

**Round** suits conversation and small rooms, and it is constrained by material: a round top needs a genuinely wide board or a deliberate join. Olive and other short, wide species suit round work well.

## The live edge as a design element

On a long dining table, much of the perimeter is hidden by chairs and seated guests. A dramatically irregular edge may be largely invisible in use.

Gentle undulation reads better on a dining table; dramatic irregularity is better spent on a console or coffee table where the whole outline is visible. [The art of the live edge](/blog/the-art-of-the-live-edge) covers how edge character varies.

## Light and placement

Consider where daylight falls. A resin river reads completely differently under raking light than under flat overhead light, and a table positioned to catch morning light will look like a different object at three different times of day.

Also consider what you are placing it near. Sustained direct sunlight ambers resin, and radiators and underfloor heating cause the timber and resin to move against one another. [Care and placement](/blog/how-to-care-for-a-resin-river-table) covers this properly.

## Access

Measure the route in. Doorways, stair turns, lift dimensions, and corridor corners have defeated far more table deliveries than freight damage.

A 260cm crated table does not go round a tight landing. Establish this before commissioning, not on delivery day — [shipping and handling](/blog/international-shipping-and-handling) explains what to check.

## Bringing it together

Room dimensions and access first. Then width and length. Then base, shape, and species.

Aesthetics last, because aesthetics are the part that can flex once the constraints are known.
