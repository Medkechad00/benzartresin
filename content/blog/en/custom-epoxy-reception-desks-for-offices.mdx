A reception desk is the first physical object a visitor touches in a building. It is also a workstation that someone occupies for eight hours a day.

Those two requirements pull in different directions, and a desk that satisfies only the first is a bad piece of furniture.

## Why this application suits the material

Of all commercial uses, reception is the strongest fit.

Physical wear is moderate — nothing like restaurant service. Visibility is maximal. And the signalling function is real: a bespoke single-slab desk communicates something about an organisation that a laminate system cannot, at a cost that is trivial against a fit-out budget.

## What has to be designed in

### Cable management

Power, data, phone, and increasingly device charging all have to arrive at the working surface without visible cable runs.

This means designing routes through or behind the structure, positioning grommets where equipment will actually sit, and specifying capacity beyond current need. Retrofitting a cable route into a cured resin top is genuinely difficult and never looks deliberate.

Tell us the equipment schedule at design stage. Where the monitor goes determines where the grommet goes.

### Height and accessibility

Reception desks generally need two working heights: a standing-height transaction counter for visitors, and a seated-height work surface behind it.

They also frequently need an accessible-height section — a lowered portion of counter usable by a wheelchair user. Requirements vary by jurisdiction and by the applicable building regulations, so confirm what applies to your project with your architect or building control. It has to be part of the geometry from the start.

### Equipment integration

Card readers, visitor management tablets, signature pads, printers, and monitors all need considered positions. So does storage — reception staff need somewhere for the things that must not be visible.

### Sightlines

A transaction counter that is too high isolates staff from visitors. Too low and staff feel exposed and their screens are visible. This is worth resolving on a drawing before it is resolved in walnut.

## Specification differences from a dining table

**Finish.** A film finish — polyurethane — rather than an oil. Reception surfaces meet hand sanitiser, coffee, pens, and cleaning contractors with commercial products. Film resists all of it better. The cost is local repairability. [The full comparison](/blog/epoxy-vs-polyurethane-table-finish) covers the trade-off.

**Scale and joins.** Reception desks are frequently longer than any single slab. That means joins, and joins should be designed as visible, deliberate features rather than disguised. A well-executed join with matched grain reads as craft; a badly disguised one reads as a defect.

**Structure.** A desk carries equipment, leans, and sometimes gets sat on. The substructure is engineering, not furniture-making, and it is designed accordingly.

**Modularity.** Long desks may need to arrive in sections to get through the building. Establish the access route early — this constrains the design more than most clients expect.

## Design considerations that pay off

**Lighting.** Reception areas are usually lit deliberately. A resin river under directional lighting is dramatic; under flat panel lighting it can look inert. Coordinate with the lighting design.

**Sunlight.** Many receptions are heavily glazed. Sustained direct sun ambers resin over years. If the desk faces a glazed façade, discuss orientation and any solar control before design freeze — [care and placement](/blog/how-to-care-for-a-resin-river-table) explains the mechanism.

**Brand fit.** Resin colour can be matched to a brand palette, and this often works better as a restrained accent than as a full-saturation match. [Resin colours and finishes](/blog/resin-customization-colors-and-finishes) covers what different treatments do.

**Front texture.** The visitor-facing fascia is a non-working vertical surface, which makes it the right place for [sculpted or textured resin](/blog/sculpted-and-textured-resin-surfaces) — tactile and visible, without compromising the work surface.

## Programme

Commercial projects run to programme, and the build does not compress to fit. The standard 8–12 weeks plus freight applies, and slab sourcing can extend it if dimensions are demanding. [the custom table timeline](/blog/from-consultation-to-shipping-timeline) sets out the stages.

Build in contingency. Fit-out dates move; resin cure times do not.

## Beyond reception

The same considerations apply to boardroom tables, executive desks, and meeting furniture. Boardroom tables raise the join question most acutely, since the sizes involved always exceed a single board.

Where several pieces need to relate visually, material has to be secured together at the outset — [matching sets](/blog/matching-sets-dining-coffee-side-tables) explains why this cannot be done retrospectively.
