"Traditional craftsmanship meets modern technology" is a claim almost every furniture studio makes, and it usually means nothing.

Here is what it actually means in our case, and where the limits of the claim are.

## The tradition that exists here

Marrakech has a genuine and continuous history of architectural surface craft. Not furniture-making specifically — surface work.

Carved cedar ceilings. *Zellij* tilework, where geometric patterns are assembled from individually cut pieces. *Tadelakt*, a polished lime plaster burnished with stone to a waterproof sheen. Incised plasterwork. Marquetry in thuya and lemonwood.

What unites these is that they are all about what happens when a hand tool meets a surface repeatedly, deliberately, over a long period. The tradition is one of patience with surfaces.

## What transfers

That is the part that carries over, and it is more specific than it sounds.

**Reading a surface by hand.** The most consequential stage in making a river table is the hand-sanding of the wood–resin interface, because the two materials abrade at different rates. A machine pass leaves the transition proud or dished. You find it by touch, not by eye — and you can feel a bad transition long before you can see it. That is a trained skill, and it is the same skill a *tadelakt* worker uses judging when a surface is ready.

**Knowing when to stop.** Over-working a surface is as damaging as under-working it. Traditional crafts teach this because the materials punish it.

**Tolerance for slow stages.** Resin cures on its own schedule. A workshop culture built around processes that cannot be hurried adapts to that more easily than one built around throughput.

**Hand-shaping cured material.** [Sculpted and textured resin work](/blog/sculpted-and-textured-resin-surfaces) is carving. The tools differ from those used on cedar; the approach does not.

## What does not transfer

We should be equally clear about this.

Epoxy chemistry is not traditional knowledge. Deep-pour formulations, exotherm management, degassing, UV inhibitors, and cure schedules are modern materials science, and they have to be learned from the material and its manufacturers rather than from a workshop lineage.

Moisture metering, kiln scheduling, and dimensional stability are technical disciplines. So is engineering a base that will carry a heavy top without racking.

Nor is the *form* traditional. A live-edge river table is a contemporary Western furniture type. We are not making Moroccan furniture; we are making contemporary furniture in Morocco, using a local craft sensibility.

Claiming otherwise would be a nicer story and a false one.

## Why the location genuinely matters

Three real reasons, none of them romantic.

**Skilled hand labour is available.** The finishing stage of a river table is enormously labour-intensive, and it cannot be automated. Being in a place where hand-finishing skills are common and where that work is respected is a practical advantage.

**Material access.** Atlas walnut and Moroccan olive are locally available in ways they are not elsewhere. When the right local board appears, we use it. When it does not, we source elsewhere and say so.

**Cost structure.** Being candid: the same level of hand-finishing in Western Europe or North America would price these pieces considerably higher. The advantage is passed into bench hours rather than into margin — it is why we can justify the finishing time that [the process](/blog/epoxy-resin-live-edge-wood-craftsmanship-guide) requires.

## Where the sensibility shows

**Surface over form.** Our pieces tend to be restrained in silhouette and highly worked in surface. That is a direct inheritance.

**Comfort with irregularity.** Traditional Moroccan work does not pursue machine uniformity. Slight variation is evidence of a hand. That maps naturally onto [live-edge work](/blog/the-art-of-the-live-edge), where the material's irregularity is the entire point.

**Light.** *Zellij* and carved plaster are designed for raking light — they are relatively inert under flat illumination and come alive at an angle. We think about resin the same way, which is why we ask where the daylight in your room comes from.

## The honest summary

The chemistry is modern. The form is contemporary. The hands, the patience, and the attitude toward surfaces are local.

That is a smaller claim than the usual one, and it is true.
