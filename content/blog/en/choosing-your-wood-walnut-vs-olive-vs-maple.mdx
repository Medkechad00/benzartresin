Species is the decision that most shapes the finished piece. Resin colour can be changed at the design stage; the timber sets the character, and it is chosen first.

Here is how the three we work with most actually differ.

## Walnut

**Colour:** Chocolate to purple-brown heartwood, often with dramatic pale sapwood at the edges.
**Grain:** Straight to gently wavy, with frequent figure at crotches and stumps.
**Hardness:** Moderate — dents more readily than maple but resists splitting well.

Walnut is the default for a reason. It is stable, it machines predictably, and its darkness makes almost any resin colour work. Clear resin over walnut reads as deep water. Black resin makes the river disappear into shadow. Metallics glow against it.

The sapwood question matters. Many buyers want it removed for uniformity; others want it kept for contrast. It is a genuine aesthetic choice and worth deciding early, because it changes which slabs suit you.

**Best for:** dining tables, anywhere the table is the visual anchor of a dark or neutral room.

## Olive

**Colour:** Cream to golden-tan with intense, irregular dark streaking.
**Grain:** Wild, swirling, frequently interlocked. Almost never straight.
**Hardness:** Hard and dense.

Olive is the most striking of the three and the most constrained. Olive trees are not tall or straight, so large clear boards are genuinely rare. Widths and lengths that are routine in walnut may simply not exist in olive.

Its wildness is the point. The grain does not read as pattern so much as turbulence, and it makes a small table look important. It is also the species most likely to contain voids and inclusions, which is either a feature or a problem depending on your intent — many of the best olive pieces use resin to fill what the tree left behind.

**Best for:** round and organic-edged pieces, coffee tables, side tables, statement consoles. Large dining tops only when the right board appears.

## Maple

**Colour:** Near-white to pale cream, occasionally with grey mineral streaking.
**Grain:** Fine and even. Spalted maple carries black demarcation lines from fungal activity.
**Hardness:** The hardest of the three. Resists denting well.

Maple is the blank canvas. Against pale timber, a coloured resin river is unmissable — emerald, teal, and deep blue all read strongly against maple in a way they cannot against walnut.

Spalted maple is a separate proposition: the black spalting lines are unrepeatable, and because spalting is caused by fungus the wood must be stabilised carefully or it will be punky in places. Well-selected spalted maple is spectacular.

Its weakness is light sensitivity. Pale woods show marks more readily, and maple ambers slightly over years of UV exposure.

**Best for:** bright and contemporary rooms, strongly coloured resin, spaces where you want the table to feel light rather than heavy.

## Quick comparison

| | Walnut | Olive | Maple |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tone | Dark brown | Golden, streaked | Near white |
| Grain | Moderate figure | Wild, turbulent | Fine, even |
| Hardness | Moderate | Hard | Hardest |
| Large slabs | Readily available | Rare | Available |
| Pairs with | Any resin | Clear, amber | Strong colour |

## Practical guidance

**Match the room, not the trend.** A dark table in a dark room disappears. A pale table in a bright room can look insubstantial. Contrast is usually what you want.

**Decide about sapwood and voids early.** They are not defects, but they are choices, and they narrow the slab search considerably.

**Let availability lead.** If you are set on olive at 240cm, be prepared to wait for the board. Being flexible on species while firm on dimensions almost always gets you a better table sooner.
