Resin is not a coating. In a river table it is a structural element with its own optical behaviour, and the treatment you choose changes the piece more than almost any other decision except the timber.

## Clear

The most requested and the least forgiving.

Clear resin behaves like still water. It transmits light through the full depth of the pour, which means everything is visible — the underside of the live edge, every void, every inclusion, any bubble that escaped the degassing stage.

That transparency is exactly why people want it, and exactly why it demands the best preparation. There is nowhere to hide.

Clear works best where the live edge itself is beautiful, because the resin's job is to display it rather than to be the feature. Over [walnut](/blog/choosing-your-wood-walnut-vs-olive-vs-maple) it reads as depth; over pale timber it can look almost like glass.

## Tinted transparent

A small amount of pigment in an otherwise transparent pour. Emerald, teal, amber, and smoke are the tones we return to most.

This is the middle path and often the right one. You keep the depth and light transmission of a clear pour, but the colour gives the river a defined presence rather than making it a void. Colour intensity increases with pour depth, so the same tint reads lighter at the edges and deeper in the middle — an effect that mimics real water and is difficult to achieve any other way.

## Opaque

Full pigment loading. No light passes through.

An opaque river reads as stone or enamel set into timber. The live edge below the surface disappears entirely, which means preparation below the waterline matters far less — but it also means you lose the feature that motivated many people to want a river table.

Black is the most common. Against charred ash it produces something almost monolithic; the piece in our collection built this way relies entirely on the contrast between brushed char and flat black.

Opaque is the right call when you want graphic impact rather than depth, or when the underside of the edge is not worth showing.

## Metallic

Mica or metallic pigment suspended in transparent resin, worked while the pour is still moving so the metallic follows the flow.

Gold, bronze, copper, and pewter all work. The effect is directional — it shifts as you move around the table and changes completely under different lighting, which is either the appeal or the drawback depending on your room.

Metallics take more bench time because the swirl has to be worked deliberately and cannot be corrected once the resin begins to gel. They pair best with wild grain like olive, where the turbulence in the wood and the movement in the resin reinforce each other.

## Suspended elements

Occasionally requested: stone, shell, dried botanicals, or fine metal leaf suspended in the pour.

We take these case by case. Anything suspended must be genuinely dry and chemically compatible, or it will out-gas into the resin or discolour over time. Organic material in particular is a risk we will usually decline unless it has been properly stabilised.

## What each costs in time

Roughly, from fastest to slowest:

1. Clear or single tint — baseline
2. Opaque — similar, slightly more forgiving of preparation
3. Metallic — additional bench hours for working the swirl
4. Layered or multi-colour — multiple discrete pours with cure time between
5. Suspended elements — slowest, and the highest risk of a failed pour

Because deep pours are layered anyway, adding colour complexity often means adding days rather than hours. [the custom table timeline](/blog/from-consultation-to-shipping-timeline) explains how pour stages fit into the overall schedule.

## Choosing well

**Look at your room's light.** A table that will be lit from one side all day will show metallic and tinted resin very differently from one under even overhead light.

**Consider what you want visible.** If the live edge is the reason you want the table, do not bury it under opaque pigment.

**Ask for samples against your actual slab.** Pigment on a white card tells you almost nothing. Pigment photographed against your board tells you everything.
