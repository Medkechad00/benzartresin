"Eight to twelve weeks" is an honest answer, but it is not a useful one on its own. Here is what actually occupies that time.

## Week 0 — Consultation

You send dimensions, seating count, budget range, and whatever visual references you have. We respond with questions: ceiling height and room proportions, whether the table will sit against a wall or float, how you will actually use it.

Nothing is charged and nothing is committed. This stage usually takes a few days of back-and-forth.

## Weeks 1–2 — Slab sourcing and quote

We search for three to five boards matching your dimensions and brief, and photograph each one raw and wetted — wetting a slab previews roughly how it will look under finish.

You choose. We issue a fixed quote against that specific board. The 50% deposit secures it.

This stage can run longer if your dimensions are demanding. Wide boards are not always in stock, and waiting for the right slab is better than forcing the wrong one.

## Weeks 2–4 — Preparation

Unglamorous and non-negotiable:

- **Moisture verification.** Wood that goes into resin above the right moisture content will move afterwards. If a board needs more drying time, it gets it.
- **Flattening.** The raw slab is milled flat and parallel.
- **Stabilisation.** Loose bark is removed, checks and voids are consolidated, and the live edge is cleaned back to sound material.
- **Sealing.** Every surface that will touch resin is sealed, or air trapped in the grain will out-gas into the pour as bubbles.

Skipping any of these produces failures that only appear months later.

## Weeks 4–7 — The pour

The form is built and sealed around the slab. Resin goes in layers, because deep pours generate significant heat as they cure and pouring too much at once causes overheating, cracking, or yellowing.

Each layer must reach a specific stage before the next goes on. That interval is chemistry, not scheduling — it cannot be compressed by working longer hours.

Pigmented and layered effects add pours. [Resin customisation](/blog/resin-customization-colors-and-finishes) covers what different treatments involve.

## Weeks 7–10 — Finishing

After full cure the piece is flattened again, since resin shrinks slightly as it hardens and the surface is never perfectly level straight out of the form.

Then hand sanding through progressive grits — the stage that consumes the most bench hours and the one that separates a good table from a mediocre one. Resin and wood sand at different rates, so this is done by feel.

Finish is applied in multiple coats with cure time between each. The edge profile and base are completed in parallel.

## Weeks 10–12 — Crating and dispatch

The table is photographed, inspected, and crated in ISPM 15 certified timber built around the specific piece. The balance falls due, and the crate is handed to the freight partner.

## Then: transit

Transit is additional to the build window and varies widely — regional deliveries are quick, intercontinental sea freight is not. Air freight is faster and considerably more expensive.

Customs clearance at destination adds time that no one can predict precisely. [International shipping and handling](/blog/international-shipping-and-handling) explains the process.

## What causes delays

In order of frequency:

1. **Slab sourcing** for unusual dimensions.
2. **Moisture.** A board that is not ready waits.
3. **Decision latency.** Time spent choosing a slab or approving a colour is time the queue is not moving.
4. **Customs**, which is outside everyone's control.

## Planning around a date

If you need a table for a specific occasion, tell us the date at enquiry rather than at ordering. We will tell you honestly whether it is achievable, and we would rather decline than promise a date we would miss.
