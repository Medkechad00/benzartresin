The craft is the same in every setting. The specification is not.

A table for a private dining room is optimised for beauty and longevity under gentle use. The same design in a restaurant faces hundreds of covers a week, aggressive cleaning products, and staff who are moving quickly. Those are different engineering problems, and pretending otherwise is how commercial installations fail.

This guide covers how the requirements diverge.

## Private dining

The classic case, and the one with the most freedom.

Here the table is the anchor of the room and usually the most expensive object in it. Specification can prioritise appearance: a hard-wax oil finish that feels like wood and can be spot-repaired, a dramatic live edge, a [clear or lightly tinted pour](/blog/resin-customization-colors-and-finishes) that shows everything.

The constraints are dimensional. Room proportion, circulation space around seated guests, and the access route for delivery matter more than material toughness. [Designing a statement dining table](/blog/designing-a-statement-dining-table) covers sizing and proportion properly.

## Restaurants and hospitality

A completely different brief.

Commercial dining means constant use, commercial cleaning products, staff under time pressure, and a surface that must look acceptable after years rather than perfect after months.

What changes:

- **Finish.** A film finish — polyurethane — rather than an oil. It resists spills and abrasion far better, at the cost of local repairability. [The full comparison](/blog/epoxy-vs-polyurethane-table-finish) explains the trade-off.
- **Dimensions.** More conservative. Thinner tops, less overhang, sturdier bases.
- **Expectations.** Commercial surfaces acquire wear. That is not a defect; it is what use looks like.

We are candid with hospitality clients about this. [Whether resin tables can handle restaurant service](/blog/can-resin-tables-handle-hospitality-restaurant-use) sets out where we think the honest limits are, including the cases where we would advise against a resin table entirely.

## Corporate and workplace

Reception desks, boardroom tables, and executive furniture are the strongest commercial fit for this work.

Unlike restaurant tables, these surfaces see relatively light physical use — but they are seen by everyone who walks in, and they carry a signalling function that ordinary furniture does not. A reception desk is often the first physical object a visitor encounters.

The practical requirements are different again: cable management, monitor and equipment integration, accessible-height sections, and dimensions that survive a fit-out programme. [Custom epoxy reception desks](/blog/custom-epoxy-reception-desks-for-offices) covers the specification detail.

Boardroom tables add their own problem — the sizes involved usually exceed any single slab, so the design has to handle joins deliberately rather than pretending they are not there.

## Hotels and lobbies

Somewhere between corporate and hospitality. High visibility, moderate physical use, but exposure to a public that has no investment in looking after the furniture.

Large lobby pieces also raise the light question sharply. Hotel lobbies are frequently glazed, and sustained direct sunlight ambers resin. Placement relative to glazing is worth deciding before the piece is designed, not after — [care and placement](/blog/how-to-care-for-a-resin-river-table) explains why.

## Coordinated sets

Dining table with matching coffee and side tables; reception desk with matching meeting furniture.

The critical point is that matching is a sourcing decision, not a finishing one. Genuine visual continuity requires boards from the same log, and that has to be secured at the outset. A "matching" piece commissioned two years later from a different tree will not match, however carefully the colour is chased.

[Matching sets](/blog/matching-sets-dining-coffee-side-tables) explains what is realistically achievable.

## What does not change

Across every setting, the underlying process is identical: proper drying, thorough stabilisation, layered pours, post-cure flattening, and hand finishing. [The craftsmanship guide](/blog/epoxy-resin-live-edge-wood-craftsmanship-guide) covers it.

The commercial pieces are not built to a lower standard. They are built to a different specification.
