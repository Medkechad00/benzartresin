Almost everything that decides whether a river table is still worth looking at in twenty years happens before a drop of resin is poured. None of it is visible in the finished piece.

Here is the whole sequence, and what each stage is actually for.

## Selecting the slab

A board is chosen for what it can become, not for how it looks in the rack. We read four things:

- **Moisture content.** Wood that goes into resin wet will move afterwards.
- **Structural soundness.** Checks, shakes, punky spots.
- **Edge quality.** The live edge is the whole feature, so a ruined edge rules a board out.
- **Geometry.** A badly twisted board loses too much thickness in flattening to survive at your dimensions.

Species then sets the character. [Walnut, olive and maple](/blog/choosing-your-wood-walnut-vs-olive-vs-maple) behave differently under tooling and under resin alike.

## Drying and acclimatisation

Wood is hygroscopic. It takes moisture from the air, gives it back, and changes dimension while doing so.

Resin does none of that. Once a slab is encased, any movement left in the timber pulls against a rigid material that refuses to move with it. Something has to give, and it is either the glue line or the wood.

So the board is dried to the correct moisture content and checked with a meter before anything else begins. If it is not ready, it waits. This is the most commonly skipped step in cheap river tables, and the most common reason they fail.

## Flattening

The raw slab is milled flat and parallel. That costs thickness, which is why a heavily cupped board can be unusable at your target dimensions even when its face measurements look generous on paper.

## Stabilisation and preparation

Before the pour: loose bark comes off, the live edge is cleaned back to sound material, checks and voids are consolidated, anything unsound is dealt with now rather than later, and every surface that will touch resin is sealed.

That last one matters far more than it sounds. Wood is full of air. Uncoated grain out-gasses into curing resin and leaves a field of bubbles along the interface, locked there permanently under a clear pour. [Preserving the live edge](/blog/the-art-of-the-live-edge) covers how much bark and natural shape can realistically survive this stage.

## The pour

The form is built and sealed. Then the resin goes in, in layers.

Curing epoxy generates its own heat, and the thicker the mass the more heat it traps. Pour too deep in one go and the centre can reach temperatures that crack, cloud, yellow, or in the worst cases scorch the resin from within.

Deep-pour formulations are engineered to cure slowly for exactly this reason, and they still have to be layered. Each layer must reach a particular state before the next one goes on. That interval is chemistry. It cannot be compressed to hit a delivery date.

Colour is introduced here. [Resin colours and finishes](/blog/resin-customization-colors-and-finishes) covers what each treatment does optically.

## Post-cure flattening

Resin shrinks a little as it hardens, so a surface straight out of the form is never truly flat. The whole top gets flattened a second time.

The timing is the point: this happens *after* full cure, not after pouring. Flatten resin that has not finished hardening and you get a surface that keeps moving once you stop.

## Hand finishing

The longest stage, and the one that separates a good table from a merely acceptable one.

Sanding climbs through progressive grits. Resin and wood abrade at different rates, so a machine pass on its own leaves the transition line either proud or dished. The interface has to be worked by hand and judged with fingertips — you can feel a bad transition long before you can see it.

Finish then goes on in several thin coats, with cure time between each. Hard-wax oil penetrates and can be spot-repaired in place. A film finish sits on top, resists more, but has to be stripped and redone wholesale when it eventually fails. Which one is right depends on use: [in hospitality](/blog/can-resin-tables-handle-hospitality-restaurant-use) the maths is nothing like a private dining room.

## Why the finish choice matters

[Epoxy versus polyurethane](/blog/epoxy-vs-polyurethane-table-finish) is the question we field most about durability. The honest answer is that they are not rivals. They do different jobs.

## Beyond the flat pour

Not every piece is a flat top with a river running through it. [Sculpted and textured surfaces](/blog/sculpted-and-textured-resin-surfaces) are shaped by hand after cure, which is slower and a good deal less forgiving.

## The point of all this

None of these stages photograph. A table with rushed drying, unsealed grain and machine-only finishing can look identical to a properly built one on the day it arrives.

The difference shows up in year three.
