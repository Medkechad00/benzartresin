Most of what determines whether a river table is still beautiful in twenty years happens before any resin is poured, and none of it is visible in the finished piece.

This is the full process, and why each stage exists.

## Selecting the slab

A slab is chosen for what it will become, not for how it looks in the rack. We assess:

- **Moisture content**, because wood that goes into resin wet will move afterwards
- **Structural soundness** — checks, shakes, and punky spots
- **Edge quality**, since the live edge is the feature
- **Overall geometry**, as a severely twisted board loses too much thickness in flattening

Species sets the character. [Walnut, olive, and maple](/blog/choosing-your-wood-walnut-vs-olive-vs-maple) each behave differently under both tooling and resin.

## Drying and acclimatisation

Wood is hygroscopic — it takes on and gives off moisture with the surrounding air, and it changes dimension as it does.

Resin does not. Once a slab is encased, any subsequent movement in the timber pulls against a rigid material that will not move with it. The result is separation at the glue line, or cracking.

So the slab is dried to the right moisture content and verified with a meter before anything else happens. If a board is not ready, it waits. This is the single most common corner cut in cheap river tables and the most common cause of failure.

## Flattening

The raw slab is milled flat and parallel. This costs thickness, which is why a heavily cupped or twisted board may not be viable at your target dimensions even if its face measurements look right.

## Stabilisation and preparation

Before the pour:

- Loose bark is removed, and the live edge is cleaned back to sound material
- Checks and voids are consolidated
- Anything unsound is dealt with now, not later
- Every surface that will contact resin is sealed

That last step matters more than it sounds. Wood is full of air. Uncoated grain out-gasses into curing resin and produces a field of bubbles at the interface — visible forever under a clear pour. [Preserving the live edge](/blog/the-art-of-the-live-edge) covers how much bark and natural shape can realistically be kept.

## The pour

The form is built and sealed. Then resin goes in, in layers.

Curing epoxy is an exothermic reaction — it generates its own heat, and the thicker the mass, the more heat it traps. Pour too deep at once and the centre can reach temperatures that cause cracking, clouding, yellowing, or in extreme cases scorching.

Deep-pour formulations are engineered to cure slowly for exactly this reason, and they still must be layered. Each layer has to reach a specific state before the next goes on. That interval is chemistry and cannot be shortened.

Colour is introduced here. [Resin colours and finishes](/blog/resin-customization-colors-and-finishes) covers what each treatment does optically.

## Post-cure flattening

Resin shrinks slightly as it hardens, so the surface out of the form is never perfectly flat. The whole top is flattened again after full cure.

Note that this happens *after* curing, not after pouring — flattening resin that has not fully hardened produces a surface that continues to move.

## Hand finishing

The longest stage, and the one that separates a good table from a mediocre one.

Sanding runs through progressive grits. Resin and wood abrade at different rates, so a machine pass alone leaves the transition line proud or dished. The interface is worked by hand and judged by touch — you can feel a bad transition long before you can see it.

Finish is then applied in multiple thin coats with cure time between each. A hard-wax oil penetrates and can be spot-repaired later; a film finish sits on top and is more resistant but must be refinished wholesale when it fails. Which is right depends on how the piece will be used — [in hospitality settings](/blog/can-resin-tables-handle-hospitality-restaurant-use) the calculation is different from a private dining room.

## Why the finish choice matters

[Epoxy versus polyurethane](/blog/epoxy-vs-polyurethane-table-finish) is the question we are asked most about surface durability, and the answer is that they are not competitors — they do different jobs.

## Beyond the flat pour

Not every piece is a flat top with a river through it. [Sculpted and textured surfaces](/blog/sculpted-and-textured-resin-surfaces) are shaped by hand after cure, which is slower and considerably more demanding.

## The point of all this

None of these stages are visible in a photograph. A table with skipped drying, unsealed grain, and machine-only finishing can look identical to a properly made one on the day it is delivered.

The difference shows up in year three.
